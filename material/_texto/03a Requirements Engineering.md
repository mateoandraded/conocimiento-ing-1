What is Requirements Engineering?
• The requirements for a system are the descriptions of the
services that a system should provide and the constraints on
its operation.
• These requirements reflect the needs of customers for a
system that serves a certain purpose such as controlling a
device, placing an order, or finding information.
• The process of finding out, analysing, documenting and
checking
these
services
and
constraints
is
called
requirements engineering.

User and System Requirements
• According to Sommerville, user requirements mean the high-
level abstract requirements (problem domain) and system
requirements mean the detailed description of what the system
should do (solution domain).
• User requirements are statements, in a natural language plus
diagrams, of what services the system is expected to provide to
system users and the constraints under which it must operate.
• System requirements are more detailed descriptions of the
software
system’s
functions,
services,
and
operational
constraints. The system requirements document (sometimes
called a functional specification) should define exactly what is to
be implemented.

Problems in Requirements Engineering
• Stakeholders don’t know what they want and/or they cannot
articulate their requirements in language the requirements
engineer can understand
▪It is difficult to visualise the end-product, particularly if innovative
• Stakeholders fail to define some requirements, because they
believe the requirement to be obvious
• Stakeholders think they know what they want until we give
them what they said they wanted
• Analysts think they understand stakeholder problems better
than the stakeholder

Communication Principles
• Before customer requirements can be analysed, modelled, or
specified they must be gathered through the communication
activity.
• Principle 1. Listen.
▪Ask for clarification if something is unclear but avoid constant
interruptions.
• Principle 2. Prepare before you communicate.
▪Spend the time to understand the problem before you meet with
others.
• Principle 3. Someone should facilitate the activity.
▪To keep the conversation moving in a productive direction; to mediate
any conflict that does occur; and to ensure that other principles are
followed

Functional and Non-Functional Requirements
• Software
system
requirements
are
often
classified
as
functional or non-functional requirements.
• Functional requirements
▪These are statements of services the system should provide, how
the system should react to particular inputs, and how the system
should behave in particular situations.
▪In some cases, the functional requirements may also explicitly
state what the system should not do.
▪Functional requirements should be expressed quantitatively
whenever possible to avoid conflict with the client.

Non-Functional Requirements
• Non-functional requirements
▪These are constraints on the services or functions offered by the
system. They include timing constraints, constraints on the
development process, and constraints imposed by standards. Non-
functional requirements often apply to the system as a whole
rather than individual system features or services.

• Product (Efficiency)
▪Efficiency requirements relate to performance and space and
answer such questions as, 'what performance criteria are required

growth affect performance or capacity’?
▪Measurable requirements to consider include:
❖Average response time for a transaction
❖Maximum response time for a transaction
❖Throughput: transactions per second
❖Capacity: the number of transactions the system should cope with
❖Screen refresh time
❖Space: Kbytes
❖Number of RAM chips

Non-Functional Requirements
• Product (Usability)
▪Usability requirements are concerned with specifying the user interface
and end-user interactions with the system.
▪Measurable requirements to consider include:
❖Hours of training required before independent use of the system (defined by
category of user);
❖Measurable task times for typical tasks (for a normal user);
❖Conforms to the standards defined in the XYZ ‘look & feel’ standard
❖Existence of user manuals, online help etc;
❖Errors made when working at a normal rate;
❖The system should be considered by x% of the user community to be as usable
as ? system;
❖Times to install/uninstall software;
❖Times to correct an error (in response to an error message - how helpful is the
message?);
❖Time to contact and receive a helpful response from the service provider.

Non-Functional Requirements
• Product (dependability) - Maintainability/Repairability
▪Maintainability requirements are concerned with the ability to
easily modify the software and carry out repairs and links to the
non-functional requirement type repairability.
▪Factors that affect maintainability include response time to carry
out change (mean time to change) and category of potential
change.
▪A measurable requirement would specify response time to carry
out a change of type: simple, moderate, complex.

Non-Functional Requirements
• Product (dependability) - Reliability/availability/robustness
▪Reliability requirements are concerned with identifying the potential types of
system failure which, may occur and defining the degree of reliability
demanded by the user for each of these circumstances.
▪In order to achieve the specified reliability, functional requirements may need
to be specified to reduce the potential for the types of system failure to occur.
▪Measurable requirements include:
❖Mean time to failure (MTTF in units of time for each type of failure)
❖Probability of unavailability
❖Rate of occurrence of failure
❖Availability (%availability between ?am and ? pm for all/% of users)
❖Time to restart after failure (MTTR in units of time for each type of failure - % of users for
whom the system is available may also be specified)
❖Percentage of events causing failure
❖Probability of data corruption on failure
❖Defect rate (defects/FP), defects per type: critical, significant, minor, etc.

Unit 4
Non-Functional Requirements
• Organisational requirements
▪Environmental: operating platform, portability
❖Number of target platforms
❖Percentage of platform dependent statements
❖Time to adapt the software to a new platform for simple, medium and complex systems.
▪Operational: delivery time & cost, how used, interoperability
❖Operational requirements may relate to the environment in which the product operates,
for example, might define the range of operating temperatures for the equipment.
❖Delivery requirements specify when the system and all associated deliverables must be
available to the stakeholders.
❖Interoperability defines how the system interacts with external systems. This may include
specification of hardware and software interfaces.
▪Development: language, design method, process standards
❖The requirements should specify any standards which must be used, for example,
communication standards (TCP/IP), platform standards (Windows, UNIX), quality
standards (ISO 9001, CMM), project management (PRINCE2).
❖The requirements could also define internal standards e.g. use of a particular CASE tool,
object-oriented development, particular programming languages or design methods.

Non-Functional Requirements
• External requirements
▪Regulatory
❖Identify any regulatory bodies which will impact on the requirements, for
example the Financial Services Authority for accounting practices,
Advertising Standards Authority which may affect web services.
▪Legislative: accounting, safety, security
❖Within legislative requirements we need to consider whether there are
any legal requirements (e.g., accounting rules), privacy (security) or health
& safety issues which will impact requirements.
▪Ethical: acceptable to customer and public
❖For example, if there are privacy issues relating to information handled by
the system, the steps to guard privacy of the individual must be specified.
For example, in insurance systems is it ethical to record private
information linked with legal risk e.g., medical history?
